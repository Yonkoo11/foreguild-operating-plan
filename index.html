Foreguild: earn fees first, let markets bind as depth grows

The revenue path.

  1. What it sells: software and data tasks that a script can accept or reject, at $40 to $100 a ticket.
  2. What it keeps: a 10% fee (band 6% to 14%), kept only after the script passes and the 14-day refund window closes. Only that retained fee counts as revenue.
  3. Break-even: 1,765 settled tasks a month at $40 and 10%, which is about $7,060 of retained fees a month against $6,000 of fixed cost. The goal is to cross that line by month 9.
  4. What futarchy tunes: fee level, bond sizes, routing and which task families open. Every proposal is judged on one thing: does it raise retained fees.

How much the market is allowed to decide.

  1. The stake cap: a market may bind a decision only if an attacker would lose more moving the price than the decision is worth. Anything bigger uses the price as advice and a slower path decides.
  2. Where that stands today (measured 14 Nov 2026): in the deepest of the 251 Seer futarchy pools that hold liquidity, moving the price 10% costs about 2,469 sDAI and loses about 115 if the price returns. So the first markets advise.
  3. How they come to bind: half of each month's surplus goes into pool liquidity. A market may bind a decision worth V once depth per side reaches 440 × V. Binding power grows only as fast as retained fees.

Every table comes from one of three scripts in the repo (section 12). Each number is labelled Fact or Assumption (section 11).

1. What the plan builds on

Three things shape the plan:

  • Seer's shipped futarchy is price-based: YES and NO token pairs trade in two pools, and it is only on Gnosis Chain. An invalid market counts as a failed proposal. (docs.seer.pm/app/futarchy-markets)
  • Reality.eth needs each new answer to post at least twice the previous bond. A Kleros dispute takes up to 14 days 12 hours, plus 11 days per appeal. (report-answer, raise-a-dispute)
  • The Futarchy Autonomous Optimizers design page describes a FAO that optimises a TOKEN/CURRENCY price and is set up with a minimum liquidity, a TWAP window and a random failure chance. I could not confirm who wrote it or whether Seer endorses it, so I treat it as a reference design. This plan maps onto it like this: the Foreguild token priced in sDAI is the TOKEN/CURRENCY pair; the time-weighted price is its TWAP window; the stake cap is its minimum liquidity, set per proposal from the value at stake instead of one fixed number. I do not use random failure in any number here.

3. Where tasks come from

Lane A: direct buyers (starts day 1). Developers and small teams with chores a script can check: failing CI, dependency bumps, data cleanup against a schema. Target ticket $40 to $100.

Lane B: mirrored public bounties. The Scout agent solves public bounties and collects the payout. poidh is one source. Its contract on Base sets a 0.001 ETH minimum bounty ($1.90 on 14 Nov) and a 2.5% fee. 4. How work is assessed and paid

  1. The buyer funds escrow. Every task has an acceptance test written at intake.
  2. The Verifier runs the test. Pass starts a 14-day dispute window; fail refunds the buyer and slashes the bond if the failure is proven.
  3. After the window closes with no upheld dispute, the worker is paid and the guild keeps its fee. Only that retained fee counts as revenue.

Disputes go to a human reviewer tier first, then to a Kleros case as the last step. Humans are paid per case.

5. How futarchy governs (and where it must not)

What futarchy decides

Fee band, bond sizes, routing rules, which task families open, and any spend above a budget envelope. Day-to-day agent decisions stay under the adopted policy file.

How a decision runs

  1. The stake V (12-month value) is computed by a published formula, not entered by the proposer: for a fee change, trailing 90-day volume × 4 × the fee change, summed over all changes to that parameter in the last 90 days so a big change cannot be sliced under the cap. For a budget, the amount. For routing and opening a new task family, V cannot be bounded, so those are advisory only. A wrong V is challengeable with a Reality.eth question: "Is the published V for proposal [id] within 20% of the formula applied to ledger data at [block]?"
  2. Two conditional pools open, as Seer's futarchy markets do. Foreguild policy (not a Seer feature): adopt only if the adopt-side price beats the reject-side price by a pass margin, as a time-weighted average over the window.
  3. The adopted policy is loaded by the agents automatically.

The stake cap

Before opening, the guild reads the pool depth D and estimates what it costs an attacker to push the price by the pass margin. A market may bind a proposal only if V ≤ attacker loss: an attack that wins the decision must cost more than the decision is worth. I assume the attacker's gain is at most V.

Measured on Seer's live pools. measure.py reads Seer's market index and the pool contracts on Gnosis and works out, pool by pool, what it costs to move the price and what the pusher loses if the price fully returns. Run on 14 Nov 2026: Seer lists 442 futarchy proposals on Gnosis; 281 of their YES/NO pools exist on chain and 251 have liquidity at the current price. The three deepest:

Ordinary outcome pools are thin too: across 575 outcome/sDAI pools, lifting a price by 2 cents costs a median of 1.43 sDAI (90th percentile 17, largest 347). Read against the stake cap: the deepest futarchy pool holding liquidity on Seer today could bind a decision worth about 115 sDAI at a 10% margin. Every Foreguild decision that matters is bigger than that, so the first markets advise. The slower path decides: a human review tier, a published reasoning note, and the 48-hour guardian veto, with the market price as one input.

The model, for comparison. model.py assumes a constant-product pool with depth D per side. Capital to move the price by margin m is D(√(1+m)−1) and the loss on full reversion is that capital × (1−1/√(1+m)). The measured pools show the same ratio (4.65% of cost at a 10% margin), so the model is a fair stand-in for a full-range pool.

The loss figure is a floor: the price is read as a time-weighted average, so an attacker keeps paying while arbitrage pulls the price back.

The ladder from advice to binding

Depth is paid for by revenue. Each month, half of the surplus (retained fees minus costs) goes into a liquidity reserve: half of it buys the Foreguild token, half stays in sDAI, and the pair is placed in the proposal pools. Liquidity is lent to a market and comes back when it resolves, so the same reserve serves one proposal after another. A market may bind a decision worth V once depth per side is at least 440 × V (10% margin). model.py, at a $40 ticket and 10% fee:

Settled tasks a monthMonthly surplusDepth per side after 12 monthsLargest decision a market may bind
1,765 (break-even)$1$3$0

That is a slow ladder. I think any FAO on today's pools faces the same one. In year one the markets earn their keep as a priced forecast of retained fees that the review tier has to answer in writing when it disagrees. Binding starts with small, bounded items such as a single budget line, and widens only as fast as fees fund depth. Outside liquidity would speed it up; the rule does not change, only D.

Two gaps in the cap. The reserve buys the token, so wash fees lift the token price directly, which can be cheaper than the capital-to-move-price figure, and the tables ignore it. And V for a fee change is near zero until 90 days of volume exist, so until then every market is advisory, whatever its pool size.

Which Seer product this uses

The decision markets are Seer's shipped price-based futarchy: YES and NO pools for a Foreguild token, backed by retained fees. Fees reach the token price through a design choice of mine, not a Seer feature: the liquidity reserve above buys the token each month out of surplus, so a change in retained fees moves the token's value. The stake-cap model above describes exactly those pools. The retained-fee question in section 6 is a second, slower market that audits whether the token price tracked revenue. If it drifts, the guild stops trusting price markets until depth recovers. Whether Seer can host a revenue-valued market directly is unverified, so nothing in sections 4 to 5 depends on it.

Humans

A guardian multisig can pause payouts and veto a proposal within 48 hours. It cannot move treasury funds or write policy. Humans arbitrate last-tier disputes.

6. The audit metric and its oracle question

Seer's docs describe multi-scalar markets, where each outcome has its own numeric question (create-a-market). I did not find a page showing a futarchy proposal settled against such a number. If that is not possible, the guild publishes the same event sum and anyone can recompute it.

Metric: retained fee revenue over a 30-day period, counting fees whose 14-day dispute window closed inside the period with no upheld dispute. A decision's effect is read about 45 days after it is adopted (30-day period plus the window) when no dispute is open; a dispute pushes that back by up to the Kleros times above. So one decision area can run about 8 proposals a year at best. A 90-day metric allows about 3 and 180-day about 2. (Counting arithmetic; the effect on decision quality is my judgment.)

Exact question text posted to Reality.eth (numeric answer):

What is the sum, in USDC, of the "amount" fields of all FeeRetained
events emitted by the Foreguild fee ledger contract [address] on Gnosis
Chain with a block timestamp between [start UTC] and [end UTC]?
FeeRetained is emitted by the contract only when a task's 14-day
dispute window has closed, every dispute for that task, appeals
included, is final, and no DisputeUpheld event exists for it. DisputeUpheld is emitted only by the Kleros arbitrator adapter
or the dispute-review contract. The answer is the capped total the
contract emits in its PeriodClosed event for that period (closePeriod
applies the per-cluster cap to these events, using the cluster list
whose hash is stored on-chain).
Report the sum in USDC base units (6 decimals), start inclusive and end
exclusive. The cluster-list hash can change only through a 7-day
timelocked, publicly announced update.
Answer 0 if there are none.

The oracle question is meant to be mechanical: the answer is a number the contract emits, so an answerer has little to interpret. What a challenger would contest is the cluster list and which events count. Settlement and verification fees emit the same event; bench fees are not in the metric, because they are not tied to a task that settled. Verification fees also pay for review and Kleros costs, so the metric measures retained fees. It does not measure margin. Bond ladder (Fact: each answer needs at least twice the previous bond). With a first bond of $100, a wrong answerer who keeps defending to round 8 posts the even rounds: $100 + $400 + $1,600 + $6,400 + $25,600 = $34,100. Under Seer's rule the losing side forfeits its bonds to the winner, so that money is lost if the answer is overturned. Seer's docs also let a participant request Kleros arbitration by paying a fixed fee set by Kleros, and state that after a request "the market will no longer accept new answers". So an honest challenger can stop the ladder at any round for the price of that fee, which makes $34,100 the most a wrong answer can cost its defender.

The first bond is a flat $100 if Seer lets the asker set it (not confirmed), since a market-bound decision is small anyway (section 5). Forcing an "invalid" result, which counts as a failed proposal, costs an attacker the same ladder, so it is no cheaper than lying about the answer.

7. Self-dealing

A trader holding the adopt side could push volume through the guild to lift the metric. The defences, and their limits:

  • The metric is retained fees. To lift it by $X the attacker must pay $X in fees that stay retained. If the attacker also runs the worker side, they recover the worker payouts but not the fee. They may also recover part of it if they hold guild tokens, because the liquidity reserve buys the token out of surplus, so the net cost is $X minus that gain.
  • A per-cluster cap: no funding cluster counts for more than 10% of the metric. In a synthetic test (Pareto buyer sizes, one attacker adding 30% of fees through one cluster, one random seed), the attacker paid $221 in fees and $74 counted. Limits: the cap is measured against total fees including the attacker's uncapped ones, so the $74 is about 12% of counted fees ($74 of about $590), not 10%, and the test's 0.0% honest-fee loss is an artifact of that definition, not a property of the cap. With no attacker, the cap clips 70% of honest fees at 3 buyers, 28% at 10, 16% at 20, 8.5% at 50 and 4% at 200 (model.py, section 5). So the cap switches on only at 50 or more distinct buyers; below that it is off and futarchy stays advisory. An attacker who splits fees across enough unrelated-looking wallets to keep each under the cap passes it, so it only slows an attacker down. The main defence is the fee cost itself, plus a cluster list published on-chain with its hash so anyone can challenge it.
